\begin{abstract}
Graph neural networks are justified by a \emph{homophily} assumption: nodes joined by an edge
tend to share a label. In a graph convolutional network (GCN) that assumption is buried inside
the architecture, so we never see what it is worth on its own. This project takes the assumption
out of the architecture, writes it down as an explicit pairwise Markov random field (MRF) over
the labels, and measures under controlled conditions when stating it explicitly actually helps.
We compare three ways of imposing it: a Laplacian smoothness penalty added to the training loss
(the model in our proposal), an expected rule-violation penalty, and a Potts MRF whose unary
potentials are the frozen network's logits and whose posterior we compute with mean-field
variational inference, loopy belief propagation and Gibbs sampling -- all validated against
\emph{exact} marginals from variable elimination. We ran 7{,}744 model runs and 11{,}280
exact-inference comparisons under a protocol fixed in advance. First,
our pre-registered hypothesis that the prior helps most when labels are scarcest is
\emph{refuted and reversed}: the benefit grows with supervision ($-7.07$ accuracy points, 95\%
CI $[-10.26,-3.91]$), because an MRF propagates label information rather than creating it.
Second, on synthetic graphs where we control how true the assumption is, a prior whose strength
is fixed in advance loses up to 45 points through posterior collapse, while the same prior tuned
on validation data never loses more than 0.5 -- misspecification is dangerous only when the
model is committed to it. Third, a rewiring control shows the Laplacian penalty keeps most of
its benefit on a graph with no label structure at all, so it works largely as a generic
regularizer rather than as relational reasoning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Where this project started, and why the question needed sharpening}

In node classification the observations are not independent: whether a paper belongs to
``neural networks'' or ``genetic algorithms'' is predictable from the topics of the papers it
cites. The assumption encoding that dependence is \emph{homophily} -- an edge is evidence that
two nodes share a label.

Our proposal began from a natural idea. A GCN \citep{kipf2017semi} uses homophily implicitly, by
averaging each node's representation with its neighbours'. Why not state the assumption
explicitly, as a penalty added to the training objective that pushes connected nodes towards
similar predicted label distributions? The proposal called this a ``logic regularizer'' -- a
differentiable version of the rule $\textsc{Edge}(i,j)\Rightarrow\textsc{SameClass}(i,j)$ -- and
asked whether it helps when labelled nodes are scarce.

Two problems make that comparison hard to interpret, and both shaped our final design.

\textbf{The baseline already contains the assumption.} A graph convolution is provably a form of
Laplacian smoothing \citep{li2018deeper}. Adding a smoothness penalty to a GCN therefore asks
``does more of the same help?'', not ``does the prior help?''. Whatever we measured, we could not
attribute it. Our fix is to always measure the prior's effect twice: once on top of a GCN, where
it is a marginal addition, and once on top of a plain MLP that ignores the graph entirely, where
it is the \emph{only} source of relational information.

\textbf{The penalty is not actually a probabilistic model.} The proposal described it as imposing
an MRF on the labels, but no MRF was written down and no posterior computed -- it is a heuristic
on the network's output vectors. Since CMPT 727 provides the tools to write the real model and
do real inference in it, we did that; the distinction turned out not to be cosmetic
(\S\ref{sec:mech}).

\subsection{What we did, and what the course contributed}

We hold the network fixed and vary only how the assumption enters (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}):
as a \textbf{training-time penalty} on the parameters, or as an \textbf{inference-time Potts
MRF} whose posterior we compute over the labels. We then ask three questions, each with a
controlled experiment: (Q1) does the prior help most when labels are scarce? (Q2) how does its
value depend on whether the assumption is actually \emph{true} of the graph? (Q3) does it matter
which mechanism we use? A fourth question runs underneath: is our approximate inference accurate
enough that the answers describe the \emph{model} rather than the \emph{algorithm}? We answer it
by computing exact marginals on small graphs and scoring every algorithm against them.

The project was deliberately designed so that the course's machinery is the object of study
rather than incidental tooling. Table~\ref{tab:concepts} maps each concept to what we
implemented and the source we followed.

\section{Background}

\paragraph{Setting.} Given a graph $G=(V,E)$, features $X$ and labels $y_i\in\{1,\dots,C\}$
observed on a small subset of nodes, we predict the rest. The setting is \emph{transductive}:
all features and edges are visible during training, only labels are hidden. \emph{Edge
homophily} $h$ is the fraction of edges whose endpoints share a label ($0.81$ on Cora); since it
is not comparable across graphs with different class counts, we also report adjusted homophily
and label informativeness \citep{platonov2023characterizing}.

\paragraph{Why a GCN already assumes homophily.} A two-layer GCN computes
$H^{(\ell+1)}=\sigma(\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\tilde{A}\tilde{D}^{-1/2}H^{(\ell)}W^{(\ell)})$, replacing
each node's representation by a weighted neighbourhood average --- Laplacian smoothing
\citep{li2018deeper}. The prior is already in the architecture, which is the confound described
above.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.} None of the individual components is new;
Table~\ref{tab:concepts} names the source for each. Homophily as a label MRF with observed
labels as evidence is \citet{zhu2003semi}; a logical rule as a differentiable loss is
\citet{hu2016harnessing} and \citet{xu2018semantic}; applying a relational correction after
training a simple predictor is \citet{gasteiger2019predict} and \citet{huang2021combining};
combining a GNN with an MRF trained by EM is GMNN \citep{qu2019gmnn}. Our contribution is not a
new model but a controlled measurement of when these pieces pay off, plus a demonstration that
one widely used piece does not do what it is usually said to do.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{The experimental pipeline}

One run of our pipeline does the following.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item Sample $m$ labelled nodes per class, stratified, with fixed validation and test sets.
\item Train an encoder -- an MLP (features only) or a GCN (features and graph) -- with
      cross-entropy, optionally adding one of the two prior penalties.
\item \textbf{Save the trained network's logits $s_i(c)$ to disk.}
\item Build a Potts MRF using those saved logits as unary potentials and compute its posterior
      by approximate inference.
\item Record accuracy, macro-F1, NLL, Brier score and diagnostics as one row of a results file.
\end{enumerate}

Step 3 is what makes the comparison clean: because inference consumes \emph{frozen} logits, the
MRF and the plain network see exactly the same evidence, so any difference between them is caused
by the prior and the inference, not by a differently-fitted network.

\subsection{Three ways of imposing one assumption}
\label{sec:ladder}

Let $p_i=\mathrm{softmax}(s_i)$ be the predicted label distribution at node $i$.

\textbf{(M2) Laplacian penalty} -- the model from our proposal, penalizing disagreement across
each edge, with $\lambda$ playing the role of a prior precision in a MAP reading:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\rm lap}=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\|p_i-p_j\|_2^2,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm CE}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\rm lap}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{(M3) Expected rule violation.} If predictions are treated as independent label
distributions, the probability that two adjacent nodes share a label is
$\Pr(y_i=y_j)=\sum_c p_{ic}p_{jc}=p_i^\top p_j$, so the probability the rule is \emph{violated}
is $1-p_i^\top p_j$. This gives a loss with a direct probabilistic reading
\citep{xu2018semantic}:
$\mathcal{L}_{\rm rule}=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}(1-p_i^\top p_j)$.

\textbf{(M4) An explicit Potts MRF.} Rather than nudging outputs, we write a distribution over
the labels themselves, using the trained logits as unary evidence:
\begin{equation}
p(y\mid X,G)\;\propto\;\exp\Big[\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{i\in V} s_i(y_i)}_{\text{network evidence}}
\;+\;\beta\!\!\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\!\!\underbrace{\mathbf{1}[y_i=y_j]}_{\text{homophily prior}}\Big].
\label{eq:potts}
\end{equation}
Here $\beta\ge0$ encodes how strongly we believe the assumption, and $\beta=0$ recovers the plain
network exactly -- which we use as a correctness test. Prediction means computing posterior
marginals $q_i(c)\approx p(y_i=c\mid X,G)$ and taking the argmax.

\subsection{Proposition 1: what the three share, and where they differ}

The three are more closely related than they appear, and the difference explains one of our main
results. With \emph{edgewise agreement} $A(p)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}p_i^\top p_j$:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\rm rule}&=1-\tfrac{1}{|E|}A(p), \qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\rm lap}=\tfrac{1}{|E|}\!\!\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\!\!\left(\|p_i\|^2+\|p_j\|^2\right)
-\tfrac{2}{|E|}A(p),\\
\mathcal{F}_{\rm MF}(q)&=\textstyle\sum_i q_i^\top s_i+\beta A(q)+\sum_i H(q_i).
\end{align}
\emph{Proof.} Expand $\|p_i-p_j\|^2=\|p_i\|^2+\|p_j\|^2-2p_i^\top p_j$ and sum over edges;
$\mathcal{F}_{\rm MF}$ is the evidence lower bound for Eq.~\ref{eq:potts} under a factorized
$q(y)=\prod_i q_i(y_i)$. $\square$

All three are driven by the \emph{same} statistic $A$, but differ in two ways. First in
\emph{what is optimized}: the penalties change network parameters during training, while the MRF
changes the posterior at test time with the network fixed. Second in the \emph{companion term}:
the Laplacian form carries an extra $\sum_i\deg_i\|p_i\|^2$ that penalizes \emph{confident}
predictions regardless of what the graph says, whereas the variational objective carries an
entropy term. Section~\ref{sec:mech} tests this prediction experimentally and confirms it.

\subsection{Computing the posterior: three approximations and one exact answer}
\label{sec:inference}

Exact marginals of Eq.~\ref{eq:potts} are intractable on Cora ($7^{2708}$ configurations), so we
implemented three approximate algorithms from the course, plus exact inference for validation.

\textbf{Mean-field variational inference} (our primary engine) approximates the posterior by a
factorized $q(y)=\prod_i q_i(y_i)$ and maximizes the ELBO by coordinate ascent:
\begin{equation}
q_i(c)\;\propto\;\exp\Big[s_i(c)+\beta\sum_{j\in N(i)}q_j(c)\Big],
\label{eq:mf}
\end{equation}
so each node's belief is its own evidence plus the summed beliefs of its neighbours. We update a
whole \emph{graph-colour class} at a time: same-colour nodes share no edge, so they update
simultaneously while the step remains exact block coordinate ascent -- vectorized \emph{and}
provably ELBO-monotone, which we assert at runtime. We always run to convergence and never tune
the iteration count, since a half-converged approximation would confound ``the model is worse''
with ``we stopped early''.

\textbf{Loopy BP} is \emph{exact on trees}, giving a strong correctness test, and approximate
(sometimes non-convergent) on cyclic graphs. We use damped updates and exploit the Potts
structure for $O(C)$ rather than $O(C^2)$ messages.

\textbf{Gibbs sampling} resamples an entire colour class at once, valid because same-colour
nodes are conditionally independent \citep{gonzalez2011parallel}. We use the Rao-Blackwellized
estimator -- averaging conditional probability vectors instead of counting label frequencies --
and monitor split $\hat{R}$ and effective sample size, refusing to trust runs that fail those
diagnostics.

\textbf{Exact inference.} On small graphs we compute true marginals two independent ways --
enumeration when $C^n\le5\times10^6$, repeated variable elimination with a min-fill ordering
otherwise -- and require agreement to $10^{-10}$ before using either as ground truth.

\subsection{A synthetic graph where we control whether the assumption is true}
\label{sec:csbm}

Real datasets cannot tell us when a prior fails, because their homophily is fixed. We therefore
generate graphs from a \emph{contextual stochastic block model} \citep{deshpande2018contextual}:
$n=2800$ nodes in $C=7$ equal classes, edges drawn with probability $p_{\rm in}$ within a class
and $p_{\rm out}$ across classes, and class-conditional Gaussian features
$x_i\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{y_i},\sigma_x^2 I)$.

Setting $p_{\rm in}=hk/(n_c-1)$ and $p_{\rm out}=(1-h)k/(n-n_c)$ makes the target homophily $h$
adjustable while holding the expected degree at $k=8$, so degree cannot confound the comparison.
Knowing the generative process also gives two reference ceilings: the Bayes-optimal feature-only
classifier (\textsc{Oracle-F}, nearest class mean) and an oracle that also uses the graph with
the \emph{generator-implied} coupling $\beta_{\rm gen}=\log(p_{\rm in}/p_{\rm out})$
(\textsc{Oracle-G}). The feature noise $\sigma_x$ was fixed once, on separate pilot graphs never
reused, by requiring \textsc{Oracle-F} accuracy in $[0.70,0.80]$.

\subsection{Two ways of setting the prior's strength}

One design decision determines whether harm is observable at all. If $\beta$ is chosen on
validation data then, because $\beta=0$ recovers the baseline, a wrong prior is simply tuned away
and can never hurt. We therefore study two regimes: \textbf{tuned}, with $\beta$ selected per
condition (the prior as an \emph{option} the model may decline), and \textbf{dogmatic}, with
$\beta$ fixed at the value selected on a highly homophilous reference graph and then applied
everywhere (the prior as an \emph{assumption} committed to in advance, which is the situation of
a practitioner who believes ``linked nodes share labels'' and does not re-check).

\subsection{Correctness tests run before any experiment}

Because nearly every conclusion depends on code that is easy to get subtly wrong, we gated the
study on tests that had to pass first: $\lambda=0$ reproduces the plain GCN to $<10^{-5}$;
$\beta=0$ reproduces $\mathrm{softmax}(s)$ to $2.1\times10^{-14}$ for every engine; enumeration
and variable elimination agree to $<10^{-10}$; BP matches exact marginals on trees; the
mean-field ELBO never decreases; and permuting test labels leaves training output bit-identical,
proving test labels cannot leak into training.

\section{Experimental setup}

\paragraph{Datasets.} Cora and CiteSeer \citep{sen2008collective,yang2016revisiting} in the
standard Planetoid split, plus the synthetic generator above. Table~\ref{tab:data} reports
statistics recomputed from the loaded data rather than quoted from other papers.

\paragraph{Protocol.} The protocol was version-controlled \emph{before} any experiment ran, so
the three main comparisons could not be adjusted after seeing results. It fixes: label budgets
$m\in\{1,2,5,10,20\}$ per class, stratified and nested across $m$; 10 seeds (30 for the
confirmatory comparisons); backbone hyperparameters tuned once per dataset and architecture at
$m=5$, then frozen; prior strength tuned per condition on validation with an equal budget of six
configurations for every model; early stopping on validation accuracy. Model selection is a pure
function of validation accuracy and never reads a test-set column.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
\label{sec:stats}
Every comparison is \emph{paired}: within a seed all models see the same split and the same
trained checkpoint, so per-seed differences remove split and initialization variance. We report
the mean paired difference, a 95\% bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap interval
($B=10{,}000$) and the number of seeds in which the difference was positive. Only the three
pre-registered comparisons receive a hypothesis test (Holm-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank);
everything else is an estimate labelled exploratory. On the real datasets the seeds vary only
the training sample and initialization on a \emph{fixed} graph and test set, so the intervals
describe run-to-run variability, not generalization to new graphs; only the synthetic experiment
redraws the graph per seed. In total we ran 7{,}744 model runs and 11{,}280 exact-inference
comparisons on CPU, and 13 automated audit checks pass.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Q1: the prior's value tracks the quality of the evidence, not label scarcity}

\textbf{In short: we predicted the prior would help most when labels were scarcest; it helped
\emph{least} there, and we can say exactly why.}

Our first pre-registered comparison asked whether the MRF's advantage over a feature-only MLP is
larger at $m{=}2$ than at $m{=}20$. Over 30 paired seeds the difference-of-differences is
$\theta=-7.07$ points, 95\% CI $[-10.26,-3.91]$, $p=6.6\times10^{-4}$, with only 7 of 30 seeds
in the predicted direction. The hypothesis is refuted and the effect runs the other way.

Figure~\ref{fig:lowlabel}(b) shows the two arms moving in opposite directions. Measured against a
GCN the explicit prior's benefit \emph{shrinks} with supervision ($+4.38\to+0.59$ points from
$m{=}1$ to $m{=}20$ for the Laplacian penalty); measured against an MLP it \emph{grows}
($+4.45\to+19.55$).

\textbf{Why.} The selected coupling strengths tell the story. At $m{=}1$ the MLP reaches $29.2\%$
against a chance level of $14.3\%$: there is little label signal to propagate, and validation
accordingly selects a weak coupling (median $\beta^\star=0.375$). From $m{\ge}2$ the MLP carries
real signal, validation selects the strongest available coupling, and propagation converts
feature evidence into a 19--22 point gain. An MRF \emph{amplifies} label information that is
already present; it does not manufacture it. Against a GCN the trend inverts for the
complementary reason: the GCN has already propagated. CiteSeer reproduces the pattern
independently ($+5.34$, $+12.46$, $+10.09$ points at $m=1,5,20$, 10 of 10 seeds each).

\subsection{Q2: the prior's value depends on whether it is true -- and on whether we commit to it}
\label{sec:homophily}

\textbf{In short: a prior we tune is almost never harmful, while the identical prior fixed in
advance can destroy the model.}

Here the synthetic generator earns its place: we can make the assumption true or false while
holding everything else constant. Both remaining pre-registered comparisons are supported. The
benefit grows with homophily (difference between $h=0.9$ and $h=0.5$: $+78.77$ points, CI
$[+55.53,+84.87]$, 10 of 10 seeds) and the dogmatic prior actively harms at $h=0.05$
($-29.72$ points, CI $[-31.81,-28.16]$, 10 of 10 seeds). Both survive Holm correction.

Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} holds the decisive comparison. Under the dogmatic regime the same prior
ranges from $-45.2$ to $+54.7$ points depending only on the graph; under the tuned regime the
worst cell is $-0.5$. The failure mode is not gradual degradation but \textbf{posterior
collapse}: at $\beta{=}2$ on the $h{=}0.05$ graph, $96.6\%$ of edges end with both endpoints
predicted the same label and accuracy falls to $16.9\%$, essentially chance
(Figure~\ref{fig:sens}c). The coupling term simply overwhelms the unary evidence and the MRF
paints nearly the whole graph one colour.

The practical lesson concerns \emph{model selection as a safety mechanism}: because $\beta\to0$
nests the baseline, validation retreats to a weak coupling whenever the prior is wrong, so a study
that tuned everywhere would have seen a benign heatmap and concluded, incorrectly, that a
homophily prior is harmless. Interpolating each seed's zero crossing gives a break-even homophily
of $h^\star=0.76\,[0.52,0.87]$ at $m{=}1$, $0.57\,[0.40,0.59]$ at $m{=}5$ and
$0.43\,[0.42,0.43]$ at $m{=}20$ (Figure~\ref{fig:cross}a): more supervision lets a model tolerate
a wronger prior.

\paragraph{A low-homophily graph is not an uninformative graph.} This distinction
\citep{ma2022homophily} became concrete in our data. At $h=0.05$ the generator implies a
\emph{negative} coupling, $\beta_{\rm gen}=-1.15$: neighbours are informative precisely because
they tend to \emph{differ}. \textsc{Oracle-G}, which uses the true generative model with that
coupling, reaches $77.0\%$ -- better than the feature-only \textsc{Oracle-F} at $73.4\%$ --
while our positive-$\beta$ Potts prior collapses to $15\%$. The graph carries real signal; the
\emph{same-label} assumption is simply the wrong assumption, with the wrong sign. No amount of
tuning with $\beta\ge0$ can fix that, as Figure~\ref{fig:cross}(b) shows: the selected
$\beta^\star$ saturates while $\beta_{\rm gen}$ keeps rising, and goes negative at low $h$. This
is the clearest motivation for the future work in \S\ref{sec:future}.

\subsection{Q3: the penalty and the MRF are not doing the same thing}
\label{sec:mech}

\textbf{In short: destroy the graph's label structure and the MRF's benefit disappears, as it
should -- but the Laplacian penalty keeps working, meaning it was never really using that
structure.}

Proposition~1 predicted that the Laplacian penalty does something a relational prior should not:
penalize confident predictions irrespective of the graph. We tested this with a control rather
than another accuracy comparison. We rewired Cora with a degree-preserving configuration model,
which keeps every node's degree but destroys which nodes are connected; edge homophily drops
from $0.810$ to $0.168$. If a method genuinely exploits label structure, its benefit must vanish.

The MRF behaves exactly as a relational method should: its interval covers zero at both label
budgets. The Laplacian penalty does not -- it retains a clearly positive benefit on a graph whose
edges carry no label information. Combined with Proposition~1, the interpretation is that much of
what our proposal's regularizer contributed on Cora came from its confidence-penalty term, that
is ordinary regularization, rather than from relational reasoning.

\paragraph{Two honest caveats.} First, a three-layer GCN -- which merely propagates further, with
no probabilistic machinery -- gains $+2.91$ and $+3.69$ points at $m{=}1,2$, comparable to the
explicit prior in that regime, so we do not claim the MRF beats simply using a deeper network at
very low label rates. Second, we make mechanism claims only above a measured noise floor: two
GCNs differing \emph{only} in initialization seed already disagree on $16.2\%$ ($m{=}2$),
$14.2\%$ ($m{=}5$) and $6.5\%$ ($m{=}20$) of test predictions.

\subsection{Consistency is not accuracy, and accuracy is not calibration}
\label{sec:consistency}

Rule satisfaction $R=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum p_i^\top p_j$ and the fraction of edges whose predicted
labels agree both rise monotonically with $\beta$, while accuracy rises and then collapses
(Figure~\ref{fig:sens}). At the collapse point the model is maximally \emph{self-consistent}
($96.6\%$ edge agreement) and minimally \emph{correct} ($16.9\%$): agreeing with a symbolic rule
is not a proxy for being right. The same caution appears in the probabilities. At $m{=}2$ on Cora
the MRF improves the MLP's accuracy by $11.2$ points but makes its test negative log-likelihood
\emph{worse} ($1.86\to2.86$) -- strong coupling yields confident marginals, some confidently
wrong. Only with ample supervision does probability quality improve too (at $m{=}20$, MRF(GCN)
NLL $0.978$ versus GCN $1.055$).

\subsection{Are the approximations trustworthy? Validation against exact inference}
\label{sec:fid}

Every result above depends on approximate marginals, so we measured how good they are. On graphs
small enough to solve exactly we compared all three algorithms against exact marginals across
coupling strengths and graph structures (Figure~\ref{fig:fid}, in the appendix).

The results reproduce the classical picture \citep{murphy1999loopy} and validate our
implementations. Loopy BP is exact on all three tree structures at every coupling (largest error
$4.8\times10^{-7}$, our message tolerance) and every engine reproduces $\mathrm{softmax}(s)$ at
$\beta=0$ to $2.1\times10^{-14}$. Mean-field error grows sharply with coupling everywhere
($0.19$--$0.48$ total variation at $\beta=4$), the expected consequence of asking a factorized
approximation to model strongly dependent variables. The two kinds of error are directly visible:
on the $4\times4$ grid at $\beta=1.75$ Gibbs error falls from $0.077$ to $0.007$ as kept sweeps
grow from 10 to 3{,}000, while mean-field ($0.176$) and loopy BP ($0.060$) sit at floors no extra
computation removes -- sampling error is variance and shrinks with effort, variational error is
bias and does not.

\textbf{At full scale the honest answer is that we cannot always tell.} On Cora we ran Gibbs
against converged mean-field on identical logits. Where Gibbs passed its diagnostics
($\beta\le1$) the two agreed closely (mean marginal difference $0.035$). But at $\beta=2$ -- the
coupling validation actually selects -- Gibbs \emph{failed to mix}, with $\hat{R}$ up to $2.58$
and an effective sample size of $4.7$ from 8{,}000 samples. At exactly the setting our models use
there is therefore no trustworthy sampling reference, and we report that rather than quoting
numbers from an unmixed chain: the posterior there is evidently multimodal enough to trap it.

\subsection{Robustness}

Re-selecting hyperparameters with only 5 labels per class of validation data -- instead of the
roughly 500 the standard split provides, which is arguably unrealistic when claiming a low-label
setting -- preserves the effect ($+5.64$ points at $m{=}1$ in 8 of 10 seeds; $+7.82$ at $m{=}2$
in 9 of 10). Validation also selects the largest $\beta$ in our grid in most MRF-on-MLP cells,
so our reported gains are \emph{lower bounds}.

\section{Discussion and future work}

\paragraph{On when a prior is worth stating explicitly.} The intuition we started with -- priors
matter when data is scarce -- is too coarse. Our measurements support something more specific: an
explicit relational prior pays in proportion to the relational information the base model has
\emph{not} already extracted, and it needs enough unary signal to have something to propagate.
Both conditions failed simultaneously in exactly the setting our proposal targeted (a GCN at one
label per class), which is why that was the least informative experiment we could have run.

\paragraph{On the value of writing the model down.} Turning a smoothness penalty into an actual
MRF changed what we could learn: it gave the prior one interpretable parameter $\beta$ whose
selected value we could compare against a known ground-truth coupling, made the failure mode
diagnosable as posterior collapse rather than an unexplained accuracy drop, and let us ask
whether our inference was even correct -- which a penalty term does not permit. It also gave us
the rewiring control, to us the most useful methodological outcome: a penalty described as
encoding a relational rule should lose its benefit when the relations are destroyed, and ours did
not. We suggest that control for any work claiming a regularizer encodes relational knowledge.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Ten seeds (30 on the confirmatory comparisons) give approximate interval
coverage, and on the real datasets those intervals describe run-to-run variability on a
\emph{fixed} graph and test set rather than generalization to new graphs -- only the synthetic
experiment redraws the graph per seed. We study one architecture, one pairwise prior and the
transductive setting only, and the synthetic magnitudes depend on $\sigma_x$, chosen once in
advance, which sets how much room the graph has to help. Most importantly, at high homophily the
Potts prior is correct \emph{by construction}, since the SBM's edge probabilities have exactly
the form the Potts model assumes; that sweep is best read as a controlled check of our
measurement apparatus, with the real-data results carrying claims about real graphs.

\paragraph{Future work.}
\label{sec:future}
Our clearest result points at its own remedy. The failure at low homophily is a \emph{sign}
error: the model can only express ``neighbours agree'' while the data says ``neighbours differ''.
The principled fix is to replace the fixed indicator $\mathbf{1}[y_i=y_j]$ with a learned
$C\times C$ compatibility matrix $\psi(a,b)=\exp(\beta C_{ab})$, estimated by EM with the
unlabelled nodes' labels as latent variables: an E-step computing approximate marginals with the
machinery we have already built and validated, and an M-step updating $C$. Our \textsc{Oracle-G}
measurement bounds the prize at up to 62 accuracy points at $h=0.05$. This is the direction taken
by GMNN \citep{qu2019gmnn}; what our study adds is a measured deficiency motivating it. Two
further directions follow: better inference where Gibbs fails to mix (annealing or a structured
variational family), and richer rules than a single pairwise predicate.

\section{Conclusion}

An explicit homophily prior is most useful when the base model has not already exploited the
graph and the unary evidence is informative enough to propagate; it becomes harmful when its
strength is fixed in advance on a graph that violates it, costing up to 45 accuracy points
through posterior collapse below a break-even homophily of about $0.57$ at five labels per class.
We can make these claims because the synthetic generator manipulates the prior's correctness at
fixed degree and features, the rewiring control separates relational reasoning from ordinary
regularization, and the exact-inference study certifies that the conclusions describe the model
rather than the approximation used to fit it.

\subsection{Protocol amendments}

\textbf{A1.} Our pre-registered Gibbs convergence gate included a maximum between-chain
difference in marginals. Implementation testing showed this quantity measures Monte-Carlo
precision at a finite budget rather than mixing: a 12-node cycle at $\beta=1$ reached
$\hat{R}=1.0004$ and an effective sample size of $5{,}540$ -- textbook good mixing -- yet a
between-chain difference of $0.0258$. Keeping it as a gate would have labelled well-mixed chains
as failures. It is still computed and reported, but the pass/fail decision now uses $\hat{R}$ and
effective sample size. The change was made before any experimental result existed.

\textbf{A2.} The largest coupling in our search grid is selected in most MRF-on-MLP cells. An
extension to larger $\beta$ was run afterwards and is labelled exploratory throughout.
